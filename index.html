CASE STUDY · 01 · ZYNGA

Season Pass

I designed the user experience of a monetisation and progression feature built to drive conversions, retention, and long-term player engagement for Wizard of Oz Magic Match 3.

RoleUX Designer
Timeline8 weeks
TeamArmaan Naveet, GD
Aravind Anem, PM
CompanyZynga
Problem
80% of pass purchases happened on Day 1 and almost none after Day 2, so conversions and revenue kept falling.
What I did
Designed two new passes: a revamped Daily Pass (double-reward windows, milestone chests, the Vault) and a flexible Modular Pass for new and end-of-content players.
Key decision
Stayed inside the Wizard of Oz look and the existing component library, because core players aged 50 to 60 relied on familiar patterns.
Result
+8% retention during the pass period and +4% conversions.
01ContextThe problem and the insight behind the redesign

The problem

The existing Season Pass didn't give players enough reason to buy it, stay with it, or upgrade it. Most purchases happened on Day 1, with almost nothing after Day 2.

The original pass ran 14 days. But revenue from purchasers had flatlined across iterations, conversion rates kept dropping, and fewer players were completing the pass each season.

80%of purchases on Day 1
18%on Day 2
2%on Day 12

The insight

85% of players who purchased the pass returned daily. So the issue wasn't whether players wanted the pass. It was getting more of them to buy and keep progressing past Day 1. Three things were missing:

Short-term hooksto drive engagement
Milestonesto maintain momentum
Long-term rewardscompelling enough to justify buying a pass
02SolutionTwo new passes for different player segments

The solution

I built two new pass variations for different player segments: a revamped Daily Pass and a new Modular Pass.

Daily Pass: revamping the existing pass

Short-term engagement with double rewards. For a limited time, completing a level doubles the rewards on the next tier. That creates a short window of urgency: reach the tier in time and get 2x, or it reverts to normal.

Momentum through milestone rewards. Milestone chests appear every few levels, revealing extra rewards ahead of time. Seeing what's coming keeps the goal visible and worth chasing.

A goal worth working towards: the Vault. Completing the pass unlocks the Vault at season's end, a gold reward exclusive to Premium and Elite players. For free players, seeing the Vault is a clear incentive to upgrade. For paying players, it's a reason to get more of the game's highest-value currency.

Modular Pass: a simplified, flexible pass

Active for a shorter period, mainly for players who were new or had reached the End of Content, to nudge them to keep playing.

03ResearchCompetitors, goals and constraints

Process

Research and analysis

I grounded the redesign in three questions: what business outcome are we solving for, who are we solving it for, and what's already working elsewhere in the market?

Feature goals: increase overall conversions by 8% and raise the price point of the Elite Pass. Player demographics: age, time spent in the game each day, and willingness to spend based on perks. Competitors: I played competing features (Clash Royale, Royal Match, Piggy Kingdom) to understand UX flow, priority points, and what drove player motivation.

Constraints

The game draws from the Wizard of Oz aesthetic, so the new pass had to feel like an unpretending extension of that world.

Audience sensitivity

Women aged 50 to 60 have a daily habit of playing the game. The feature had to feel like a natural evolution of something they already knew, because unfamiliar patterns risked losing them entirely.

Tech and design limits

The component library and design system couldn't be changed, and any modification risked breaking the broader system. The feature had to keep an appropriate emotional tone without introducing anything the system couldn't support.

04DecisionsThe calls that shaped the design, and how it iterated

Key decisions

Each decision sits at the intersection of player motivation and monetisation. The goal wasn't just to drive spend, but to make spending feel like a natural extension of how players already engage.

Flow and iteration

I mapped the complete feature flow, every state, transition, and edge case, so the team had a single source of truth heading into implementation. After multiple rounds of iteration, feedback, and review, I made sure all scenarios and additional surfacing points were covered.

The final deliverable included a structured handoff doc, organized so each team could jump straight to the section relevant to their work.

05OutcomesResults and what I learned

Results

8%increase in player retention during the season pass period
4%increase in conversions

Reflection

The project ended with an 8% increase in player retention during the season pass period and a 4% increase in conversions. I'm genuinely proud of those numbers, but the bigger takeaway for me was the process itself.

Designing inside a well-established game, with its own design systems and visual language, was a different challenge than starting from a blank canvas. Every decision had to work within constraints that already existed: established iconography, existing player mental models, backend tunability, and a visual language I didn't create but had to respect and extend.

That constraint was the most valuable part of the project. It pushed me to think less about "what's the ideal design" and more about "what's the right design given everything already in place." Small, disciplined decisions, like reward pairing per tier or where lock states live, can compound into measurable outcomes like retention and conversion lift.

CASE STUDY · 02 · WATER ACCESS

Waterway

I researched how unhoused people find drinking water in Pittsburgh, then designed Waterway, a system that makes free water visible, predictable and socially safe to ask for.

RoleUser Researcher and Designer
Timeline6 weeks
TeamKousalya Mohan (Research)
MethodsSurvey, role play, observation, intercept interviews, co-design
ToolsFigma
Problem
Unhoused people in Pittsburgh struggle to find drinking water, and stigma makes it hard to ask for it.
What I did
Ran the research (survey, role play, intercept interviews, co-design) and was the sole UX designer on Waterway, which makes free water visible, predictable and socially safe to ask for.
What I learned
Barriers are social, not just physical. We expected refusals, but most people were willing to help.
Result
A UX research study of how unhoused people find water, with a potential solution for the community. The focus was the research.
01ContextThe brief, what we found, and the problem

Overview

I explored how unhoused people in Pittsburgh find safe drinking water, looking at both the infrastructure and the social factors behind it. I worked on the research with a teammate and was the sole UX designer on the solution.

Key findings

Across the survey, fieldwork and co-design, four things kept coming back:

Access shifts by time and place. It improves near libraries and shelters and drops after dark or in extreme weather.
Empathy is prevalent. We expected routine refusal; the dominant pattern was generosity, not hostility.
Barriers are social, not just physical. Uncertainty about where to ask, whether it’s okay, or if a place is open mattered more than supply.
Reliability beats proximity. People only use known, trusted sources, even when a new one is closer.

The problem

Unhoused people struggle to find adequate drinking water, and the social stigma around their lifestyle limits their ability to ask the community.

Concept mapping of the wider “outdoor living” space showed hydration as its most connected issue.

02ResearchFieldwork, survey and interviews

Exploration

We reviewed existing research to anchor our work in real city data, then visited the Light of Life Rescue Mission in Downtown Pittsburgh. Access to water there is limited and tied to meal times, so guests often depend on donations to hydrate.

Survey

A Google survey of housed respondents showed how the wider community sees the problem.

  1. Social distance and safety.Almost half (47.2%) feel uneasy when approached by an unhoused person.
  2. Willingness depends on context.Many would share water, but said it would depend on context and perceived safety.
  3. Infrastructure is the barrier.Housed respondents named the lack of accessible public fountains as the biggest obstacle to refilling away from home.

Role play

Before fieldwork, we walked the same neighbourhoods without carrying water, annotating every fountain, faucet or spigot we could find.

Intercept interviews

We interviewed five groups to compare how context and social perception shape access to water: unhoused individuals, outdoor workers, outdoorsy residents, local business employees and community members.

03SynthesisJourney, affinity map and co-design

User journey

We mapped one person’s day of finding water, from first thirst to the thoughts after.

  1. Any water over clean water.When finding water is difficult, people prioritize getting any water over making sure it’s clean.
  2. Planning doesn’t remove uncertainty.Even with careful planning, there is no surety that water will be there when needed. Quick fixes don’t solve the larger problem.
  3. Social comfort shapes choices.People often pick less convenient options just to avoid embarrassment or rejection.
  4. Stressful from start to finish.The search for water is stressful and unpredictable.

Affinity mapping

We built an affinity map of how each user group (unhoused, outdoor worker, outdoorsy person, local business and community member) relates to people asking for water in public.

  1. Access shifts by time and place.It improves near libraries and shelters and drops after dark or in extreme weather, creating “reliable / sometimes / denied” zones across routes.
  2. Hypothesis overturned: empathy is prevalent.We expected routine refusal; instead most people were willing and eager to help.
  3. Barriers are social, not just physical.Uncertainty about where to ask, whether it’s okay, or if a place is open mattered more than supply.

Co-design

With unhoused participants, we mapped when and where they find or are denied water across their daily routes.

  1. Reliability over proximity.Participants only used known, trusted sources such as shelters or parks, even when a new one was closer.
  2. Temporal variation.Access changed with time and weather; after dark, fear and safety concerns limited mobility.
  3. Empathy over stigma.Community members and businesses were often willing to help when approached politely.
  4. Emotional safety matters.Asking was shaped more by predictability and dignity than by availability alone.
04DefineWhere the opportunities are, and what a solution must do

Opportunity gaps

Water inaccessibility stems less from a lack of empathy and more from predictability, familiarity and confidence.

Discoverability & visibilityPublic water points are hard to spot or trust, so people stick to known spots.
Predictability & timingHours shift with time and weather, so unfamiliar routes feel risky and people ration water.
Social comfort & confidenceUncertainty about “is it okay to ask here?” blocks people from asking, despite widespread empathy.

Criteria for solutions

Must

Make it clear when and where public water is reliably available, without prior local knowledge, and make asking feel safe and legitimate.

Should

Normalise water access as a routine action, with a consistent experience across locations.

Could

Extend approachability after dark and in poor weather, and make access points more visible in underserved areas.

05SolutionWaterway for businesses and for people without housing

Solution: Waterway for businesses

An app for restaurants and stores to provide water by logging their availability.

  • Editable timings and a way to see ratings and impact
  • A signage generator, so people without a phone can tell a place serves water

Solution: Waterway for the unhoused

An app for unhoused people with a phone to find these spots.

  • Rank each place
  • Works offline
  • Signage tells people without a phone whether a place serves water

Process

The Figma flow, from business registration to status, badge and community feedback, plus the mobile views.

06ReflectionWhat I learned

Reflection

The solution had to be simple enough that it wouldn’t feel like a chore for businesses, and for the app to work without internet. This project taught me how to approach users with empathy and to keep a system simple.

CASE STUDY · 03 · DUOLINGO

DuoConvo Cafe

For our client Duolingo, I designed an in-person pop-up and wristband system that turns language practice into real conversations between learners.

RoleProduct Designer, UX
TeamRhea Devchell (Interaction Design, UX)
ClientDuolingo
CourseCreative Technology Sprints, CMU
ToolsClaudeUnityFigma
Problem
Duolingo learners keep long streaks but doubt their practice will turn into real conversation.
What I did
Designed DuoConvo Cafe, an in-person pop-up and wristband system that turns language practice into conversations between learners.
Key insight
Engagement is not confidence: learners return out of habit, with no real-world proof that it is working.
Result
A new way of engaging Duolingo learners outside the app.
01ContextThe problem and who it affects

Overview

Duolingo has some of the most engaged users in the world, but engagement isn’t the same as confident learning.

As learners progress past the beginner stage, many stop believing their practice translates into real ability and return to the app out of habit rather than belief.

Who’s on the app

A literature review, social media and user interviews surfaced six types of learner. We focused on the two that show the problem most clearly: the Plateau’d Streaker and the Habitual Opener.

Peer Driven LearnerLearns because friends are learning, stays engaged watching their progress.
Streak ProtectorFocused on not breaking the streak, sometimes at the cost of real learning.
Pre-Trip CrammerStudies hard before travel, drops off right after.
Plateau’d StreakerExcited early on, but progress feels invisible after a few months in.
Habitual OpenerKeeps a long streak alive out of routine, unsure if they’re actually improving.
Completion Drive LearnerWants to finish the whole course, redoes lessons for a perfect score.

Personas

Plateau’d StreakerPhoebe

Opens the app with decreasing regularity as time goes on.

Phoebe got on Duolingo after noticing her friends’ streaks. She would see them practice and feel like learning too.

  • Motivated by community and competition
  • In-game rewards are no longer gratifying; she needs more of a challenge
Habitual OpenerHera

Keeps up her streak but can’t tell if she’s really improving.

Hera made excellent progress in the beginning and hasn’t missed a day since, but is beginning to doubt her learning.

  • Motivated by Duolingo’s reward and notification structure
  • Knows several words and phrases but fears she is not making enough progress as time passes
02InsightsWhat we learned and what we set out to do

What we know

  1. Competence uncertainty.Users learn specific words and phrases but don’t know if they are using them correctly. They think of Duolingo as a passive vocabulary retention tool rather than a place for active learning.
  2. No real-world transfer.Learnt language has no effect or impact outside of the app and its rewards.
  3. Social reinforcement.Real-world stimuli, like observing friends practice their lessons, keep up their streaks and earn rewards, encourage a return to the app.
  4. Habitual, not convinced.Despite long streaks, it’s difficult for users to identify if Duolingo is working. They return to the app out of habit.

What we heard

“Technically, I’m a score 10. I know some words, some phrases but I’ve never had an actual conversation in German.”Interviewee learning Spanish and German for 270+ days

While the reward system works well in the app, the same excitement does not translate to the real world, creating a disconnect and eventual abandonment of real-world application.

Opportunity

Create an experience that encourages users to come together to test and trust the languages they’ve learned.

Key 1

Optimize for word-of-mouth impressions.

Key 2

Keep the experience simple, without needing context.

Key 3

Prioritize “in-group” returning users over new learners.

Design pillars

Four pillars shaped the concept, each backed by figures from our research.

Build community and encourage conversation93% have improved learning in groups
Bring language learning outside the app76% learn better through conversations
Find a safe space for language learners75% report stronger speaking skills through interactive practice
Build confidence65% improved confidence
60% improved fluency
03SolutionThe cafe, the Hoot Band and the app

DuoConvo Cafe

An in-person pop-up experience for learners to practice their learnt language with fellow peers over some DuoCoffee.

Learners receive a wristband that identifies their learned language, so they can be matched with peers for a community-building experience.

The CafeA Duolingo pop-up with small tables, good coffee, and space to move around.
The Hoot BandA wristband that glows in the colour of your language, so matches are easy to spot.
The ConversationReal, spoken practice with another learner, not a lesson or a quiz.
The AppWhere your connections, rewards and new learning partners are waiting afterward.

How it works

In-app experience

Learners sign up, choose their language and session timing, and get a QR confirmation. The event then stays pinned in the feed, can be rescheduled, and sends a countdown reminder on the day.

Hoot Bands

Each band lights up in the colour of the language its wearer wants to practice. Tapping two bands connects the learners as friends in the app, and every new conversation earns gems.

Back to the app

Every Hoot Band connection becomes a friend in the app, a new person to learn with.

04DeliverySystem, rollout and what’s next

System map

The experience spans four parts: the app, the Hoot Band, the participant, and the operations team who run the cafe.

Campaign rollout

A four-phase campaign takes the idea from teaser to a repeatable programme. In-app messaging runs throughout, with social media and in-person added as it builds.

Phase 1Messaging“Something exciting is coming to your city”ObjectiveTeasing the community to spark curiosity and build hype.Encouragement
Phase 2MessagingCountdowns; social media marketing is amped up.ObjectiveMobilise the community and soft launch the idea of in-person interaction.Encouragement
Phase 3Messaging“Learn better with your peers over some coffee”ObjectiveConverting Duo’s virtual hub into an offline community for language practice.Confident learningEngagement
Phase 4MessagingConnecting it with local coffee vendors to keep the process repeatable, like community chapters.ObjectiveGiving learners a reason to keep coming back, to the cafe and to the app.Scalable

Setup for success

Coffee shopA familiar, low-pressure space that borrows from everyday social rituals to make language practice feel natural. Casual seating invites mingling and easy conversation starters.
Hoot BandsWristbands cut out awkward introductions and figuring out whom to speak to. Every new conversation earns in-app gems, turning real conversation into progress you can spend on lessons.
Merch storeA space to step back from mingling, browse exclusive Duo merch, and take a little Duo home.

Where next

The format is built to travel.

Any spaceThe event can be hosted in a variety of unique spaces in cities around the world.
TravelersTravelers can practice with locals during a trip, tapping into a global community of Duolingo learners.
Local hostsLocal coffee shops, libraries and university campuses host their own DuoConvo tables.

CASE STUDY · 04 · CYRTAN

Cyrtan Energy

I helped design a service and go-to-market model that turns contaminated furniture wood waste into energy, starting with one pilot hub in Millersburg, Ohio.

RoleProduct Designer
TeamRan, Eve, Keerti, Meryem, Lance
ClientCyrtan Energy
ScopeService design, go-to-market, financial model
Problem
Painted or treated furniture wood has no sustainable removal path and ends up in landfills, with disposal costing $300 to $400 per ton.
What I did
Helped design a service, go-to-market and financial model, starting with a pilot hub in Millersburg, Ohio.
Key decision
Reframed the customer as furniture finishers, a cluster of about 30 within 20 miles, so one hub could serve them all.
01ContextThe waste problem and the opening

Overview

Cyrtan Energy is a waste-to-energy company that turns organic waste into electricity and steam.

Its system is containerized, smokeless and odorless, using a patented blue-flame technology. Our brief was to find where it could start, and how to prove it works.

Why wood

Of the organic waste streams we looked at (cornstalk, coconut husk and wood), wood is the largest, the most consistently generated and the most underserved.

Wood splits into two streams. Clean, fresh wood has well-defined pathways: it is repurposed, recycled or turned into mulch. Painted, treated, engineered or metal-laden wood is rejected, and has no sustainable removal pathway. Paint, nails and glue each need chemicals, heat or manual tools to remove.

The gap

70Mtonsof wood waste generated every year
70–80%unrecyclableof that ends up in landfills
$300–400per tontipping and hauling fees drive waste removal
02StrategyWho we serve, and where

Furniture finishers

We reframed who Cyrtan should serve: furniture finishers, a primary source of contaminated wood waste.

Finishers assemble, detail, stain, seal and apply topcoats to wooden furniture. The pilot sits within a cluster of 30 of them, and a hub works for them for three reasons.

InfrastructureSmaller finishers can’t handle the waste themselves and rely on external contractors.
AggregationIndividually they don’t produce enough waste. A cluster means one hub serves all of them.
Low costFinishers are burdened by high disposal and hauling costs.

The pilot: Millersburg, Ohio

Millersburg met three criteria.

  1. ProximityAt least 30 furniture finishers within a 20-mile radius that Cyrtan can target.
  2. High disposal cost regionAround $300–400 per ton in tipping and hauling fees.
  3. Regulation-ready regionDefined permitting pathways and industrial-zoned land make deployment fast and predictable.

Value exchange

The pilot only works if every party gets something. We mapped what each side gives and gets.

With the pilot partnerCyrtan gets

A test environment, credibility to scale, and a base level of feedstock.

Partner gets

Free electricity, zero disposal costs, and sustainability branding.

With the furniture finishersCyrtan gets

A steady feedstock stream, an energy revenue opportunity, and guaranteed clients.

Finishers get

Lower waste disposal cost, operational simplicity, clear compliance and reporting, and control and predictability.

03ServiceHow the hub works, on site and off

Service design

The service has two phases: winning the client, then removing their waste.

Phase 1: Discovery and partnership

  1. Client discovers Cyrtan and inquires about the serviceCyrtan manages channels of communication.
  2. Client shares waste characteristics and locationCyrtan finds the nearest location within the 20-mile radius and ensures contamination is within 1–5%.
  3. Client receives a contract with finance options and hub locationCyrtan prepares the contract with finance options and the hub location.

Phase 2: Waste removal

  1. Client puts waste in barrels for pickupCyrtan’s hauling partner picks up the waste.
  2. Cyrtan processes the wastePelletizes and incinerates it.
  3. Cyrtan stores the energyGenerated energy goes into batteries for revenue.
  4. Client receives a certificate of removalCyrtan prepares the certificate.

Site layout

The hub is built for dense, confined industrial lots, with a total target footprint of 0.25–0.4 acres. It runs in four zones.

By-products and compliance

Every by-product has a disposal route, and every route has a permit.

Scrubber waterNeeds pH adjustment, then treated to industrial wastewater standards.
MetalsNon-hazardous. Sent to scrap metal recycling.
Bottom ashNon-hazardous. Hauled to landfill.
Fly ashPotentially hazardous. Hauled to a hazardous landfill.
Surface coatingsPotentially hazardous. Hauled to Cleveland.

Permits keep the hub inside EPA, federal and Ohio-state requirements.

Emissions permitTitle V sets air-pollution limits. Hub design targets under 6 tons of emissions a year.
Incineration permitCISWI regulates material burning. No lead or mercury-based paints are accepted.
Fire safety permitsNFPA guides fire-hazard-free operations. Dust collection and spark detection are built in.
04Go-to-marketPositioning, rollout and marketing

Positioning

Cyrtan Energy is a local, practical partner that helps wood furniture finishers reduce the cost and complexity of handling contaminated wood waste.

One anchor, one cluster, one proof case

We are not trying to win the whole market at once. We are trying to prove one focused cluster model that can later be repeated.

  1. Secure one anchor manufacturer
  2. Build a 20-mile supplier ring
  3. Launch controlled pilot operations
  4. Measure disposal savings and reliability
  5. Use the proof case to expand

Marketing plan

After securing the anchor site, we launch a 12-month marketing plan. Its objective is to build awareness of Cyrtan as a practical local waste partner for furniture-finishing clusters.

Reposition CyrtanAs a waste partner.
Build local awarenessIn wood manufacturing clusters.
Support future salesMake conversations easier to start.

Audience. Business owners and operations leaders. The priority decision-maker is highly sensitive to recurring costs and responds best to direct, practical messaging.

Channels. Focused, low-cost channels that build local trust: information sessions for site visits and introductions, email outreach for personalised conversations, a website that explains the problem, solution and service model, and a flyer for wider exposure in the cluster.

Q1Repositioning and foundation
Q2Initial targeted outreach
Q3Credibility development
Q4Consistency and expansion readiness
05Business caseMarket, revenue and break-even

Market and revenue

The Millersburg pilot targets 1,050 tons of contaminated waste a year, worth $1,449,000 in Year 1 at full capacity.

$1,260,000Waste disposal subscriptionsFlat-rate annual capacity contracts, priced at $10 per gallon
$168,000Charged battery salesStored power sold for resale rather than relying on the grid
$21,000Carbon creditsValue from diverting contaminated wood from landfills

Capital and break-even

Initial investment is $825,000. Operating expenses run to $745,000 a year, with a variable cost of $45.67 per ton. The hub breaks even at 558 tons, against 1,100 tons on the chart.

$350,000Biomass generator, the primary technology asset
$300,000Pre-processing and emissions, ensuring strict air-quality compliance
$150,000Battery storage, capturing additional value from disposal
$25,000IT and SaaS backend for digital operations

Long-term vision

Success is measured on three metrics: annual throughput (enough feedstock for continuous generation), contribution margin per ton (revenue after variable costs covers ash disposal), and regional customer capture (at least 30 finishers within 20 miles).

The Cyrtan networkA repeatable cluster blueprint that aggregates waste from manufacturers and converts it to power in containerized hubs.
Nationalized expansionA roadmap that turns regional success into a national network of independent Cyrtan hubs.
Modular deploymentStandardized container units enable plug-and-play commissioning, cutting build times from years to months.

CASE STUDY · 05 · GOOGLE MAPS

Google Itinerary: From Plan to Place

I designed a Google Maps feature that connects itinerary planning to on-the-ground navigation, so travellers no longer plan in one tool and travel in another.

RoleProduct Designer
Timeline1 week, hackathon sprint
TeamHector Ochoa, Kiruthika Raja, Rion Kurihara
ScopeUX research, interaction model, mobile hand-off
ToolsFigmaFigma Make
Problem
85% of participants use Google Maps on the ground, but their itinerary lives in Sheets, ChatGPT or screenshots.
What I did
Ran 15 interviews and designed a Google Maps feature that brings itinerary planning into navigation, including the mobile hand-off.
Key decision
Rejected directions that asked travellers to adopt a new tool mid-trip, and met them inside Maps instead.
Result
User tested with 20 people; most said they would use it.
01ContextThe problem and the opening

Overview

85% of our participants already use Google Maps on the ground, but their itinerary lives somewhere else entirely: Sheets, ChatGPT, screenshots. Planning gets tedious the moment the trip starts.

The solution brings itinerary planning into Google Maps, instead of building a new app or expecting users to change tools mid-trip. It is a new feature that connects your itinerary planning directly to navigation.

I owned the UX research and design, and built the interaction model for list-building, curation and the mobile hand-off.

Understanding the frictions

The behavioural gapPlanning is a cold cognitive state: logical, rational and relatively free from emotional influence. Travelling is a hot executive state, with high emotional activation. In the moment, users lose track of planned places and can’t always account for external factors.
Device mismatchItineraries were built on desktop tools like Sheets, ChatGPT and Notes, but 100% of on-the-ground use happened on mobile.
Real-time adaptationThe itinerary offered no way to recover once something changed. “I had a museum planned and ready, but on the day, when I checked my maps, I realised it was close. I just didn’t expect that because it was a random Tuesday.”
02ResearchInterviews, synthesis and the journey

Goals and methods

We ran 15 in-depth interviews with people aged 21–40, at CMU and beyond, focused on how they actually plan trips, not how they say they’d like to plan them. We wanted to know where the “spark” of a well-planned trip dies once the trip actually starts.

“I just gpt my itinerary and ask for famous places but when my plans change mid-trip, I have to go find places from other days and check if we can even do them again”
“I don’t plan, I just share screenshots with my friends on chat and we just go to our message to find it”
“When I’m travelling to a new place, sometimes I lose track of time and can’t do everything I want to.”
“My friends and I wing it most times, when I plan family trips I have an excel sheet or a shared notes file that everyone adds to”

What we found

  1. Most people enjoy making their itineraries.
  2. They sometimes spend too much time in one place and lose track of their planned day.
  3. They gather places from social media and add them to group lists.
  4. They save places in a wide range of tools, from sheets to messages to AI chats.
  5. Most rely on Google Maps on the day of travel.

Journey map

Pre-planningBroad discovery through AI prompts. Users are in a low-stakes exploratory state.

03StrategyThree directions, one decision

Why this landed on a Maps feature

Each rejected direction failed for the same reason: it asked users to adopt new behaviour at the moment they have the least patience for it, mid-trip. The only viable path was meeting people inside the tool they already trust and open reflexively: Google Maps.

Why Google Lists isn’t enough

Google Maps lets you save places. It doesn’t string them into a journey, preserve the intent behind why you saved them, or reduce the friction of actually getting there. Users still scroll through their list manually, searching for what they want to do and when, while the saved list just sits there.

04SolutionPlan, curate, confirm, hand off

The solution

Seamless planning and navigation for all your trips. A new feature on Google Maps that connects your itinerary planning directly to navigation.

Plan your tripBuild one list from anywhere.
Itinerary viewOn the day of travel.
Real-time adaptationBased on weather, traffic and open and closing times.
NudgeMake the most of your itinerary.

Building something separate would mean asking users to change behaviour in their highest-stress moment, so we met them inside the app they already trust, filling the gap Maps has never addressed.

Build your list

Search to manually add any location. Personalise with dates and travel mode. Bulk-import saved “want-to-gos” from Sheets, Notes, Gemini and existing Google lists. Then generate the itinerary in one click.

Curate and refine

Select or deselect places to curate the final list before optimisation begins.

Confirm travel times

View your itinerary organised by day, and see the optimised order of stops, suggested departure times, transit routes and the reasoning behind the recommended sequence. Add non-negotiable anchors or adjust timing, and the system re-optimises the rest of the schedule around your changes.

Hand-off to mobile

Access your finalised itinerary within the Google Maps mobile app, receive proactive notifications informed by real-time traffic and road closures, and get alerts to stay on schedule. If you choose to linger, override the alert and the system recalculates the rest of your trip.

05OutcomesWhat we learned and what’s next

Outcomes

This was a one-week hackathon sprint, so there are no production metrics. But the ecosystem case gives a sense of scale: even a 0.1% lift in engagement across Google’s existing Maps user base represents a $100M-level annual opportunity.

The deeper win is behavioural. 85% of our participants were already opening Maps on the ground, so this isn’t asking users to adopt something new. It removes a step they were already forced to take manually: switching apps, searching and copy-pasting addresses.

Reflection: what’s unresolved

  1. Alerts assume the user is already looking.Real-time adaptation solves the “I checked, and it’s closed” problem, but not the user who doesn’t think to check at all, the exact museum-on-a-Tuesday scenario.
  2. Bulk import assumes clean source data.Sheets, Notes and Gemini work for structured planners, but several participants described messier habits: screenshots in group chats, aesthetic-first discovery on TikTok and Instagram.
  3. We didn’t test curation in isolation.The Select and Review step assumes users want control before optimisation runs. We don’t know if that’s true, or if it’s just friction between “generate” and “done.”

What I’d do next

  1. A/B test the Review step.See whether users complete it or skip straight to “Generate my plan”, to learn whether curation control was wanted or an assumption from research.
  2. Prototype a lighter import pathfor screenshot and social-media-sourced plans, one of the most common and least supported behaviours we saw.
  3. Test the real-time nudge against a control groupwith no nudge, to see whether it reduces the anxiety we saw while navigating, or just adds notification fatigue.

CASE STUDY · 07 · OVIACT

Oviact

Oviact is a women’s health companion for PCOS. It brings symptoms, lab results and care plans into one place, so women can understand their own data and take it to their doctor.

RoleResearcher, Product Designer
TeamKousalya Mohan, Sri Bhamidipati, Sarah
Timeline2 weeks
Result2nd place, Hack-a-Startup
Problem
PCOS is lifelong, and most tools treat it as a fertility problem while day-to-day management stays fragmented.
What I did
Led research (a 30+ participant symptom board, 21+ interviews, 100+ Reddit comments) and designed the UI in two weeks.
What I learned

Result
2nd place at Hack-a-Startup.

Overview

Oviact won second place at Hack-a-Startup, run by CMU’s Swartz Center for Entrepreneurship. PCOD and PCOS management often relies on fragmented tracking and intuition, so over two weeks our team built a tracking and management tool to make long-term care more predictable.

With Kousalya Mohan, Sri Bhamidipati and Sarah, I kept coming back to one problem: PCOD management can feel confusing and isolating. We built from real, lived pain rather than assumptions, and prioritised clarity, consistency and long-term usability. I worked on the research and discovery and on the UI, which I made with the help of vibe coding.

The process wasn’t polished. We changed direction more than once and tested our thinking as much as the product. The most valuable lesson was how to frame the right problem before trying to solve it. LinkedIn post ↗

01ContextThe problem and what is missing

The problem

PCOS and PCOD are lifelong hormonal disorders. About 1 in 10 women worldwide live with them. Left unmanaged, the long-term risks are serious: 2.5 times the risk of endometrial cancer, a 47% higher risk of type 2 diabetes, and a 71% increase in infertility and conception issues.

02ResearchWhat living with PCOS looks like, day to day

How I researched it

Our team did the research and discovery in three parts, and I worked on all of them. A symptom-mapping board run in person with 30+ participants, 21+ user interviews, and a read through 100+ Reddit comments.

Designing the symptom board

We designed a “Let’s Talk Periods” board in Figma. Days 0 to 30 of the cycle run along the bottom, grouped into luteal, menstruation, follicular and ovulation. Pain runs up the side from 1 to 10, with playful anchors such as “Annoying, not deadly” and “I cannot function”. Sticker sheets, one colour per symptom, let people place what they feel and when, and blank white stickers let them write in their own.

Running it at the CMU UC

We set the board up at the CMU University Center (UC), where I talked to potential users. Participants placed stickers for the symptoms they get across their cycle, rated by how bad they are, and left sticky notes answering “How do you manage?” and “What would help you?”.

What the board showed

More than 12 symptoms came up, including cramps, fatigue, nausea, back pain, mood swings, breast soreness and bloating.

Symptoms for about 17 days a month

The map shows women experiencing symptoms for roughly 17 out of every 30 days. We drew a curve of symptom intensity over the month to make that visible.

7,140days across a 35-year reproductive span
That adds up to about 19 years of a PCOS woman’s life spent dealing with symptoms.

Interviews and online communities

We ran 21+ user interviews and read 100+ Reddit comments to see what symptom management looks like in practice. One thread, “PCOD vent – What’s been the hardest part for you?”, had passed 53K views.

What we heard

“I don’t need another tracker. I need someone to TELL ME what my symptoms mean.”

Women told us they juggle several apps, forget to tell their doctor important symptoms, and want to show patterns rather than random notes. Several said period apps assume pregnancy, not PCOS.

The gaps

Three gaps came out of the research.

Overwhelming and fragmentedSymptoms live across multiple apps, notes and memories.
Disconnected careDoctors don’t receive organised, meaningful data.
Lack of guidanceWomen need more awareness and education.
03SolutionA unified, intelligent PCOS data hub

Oviact

Oviact is a unified, intelligent PCOS data hub. It works on three layers: context-dependent, personalised insights; clinician-ready summaries; and evidence-based education and community.

One place to track every data point

A daily quick log replaces the five apps. Women rate cramps, energy and breakouts from 1 to 5 and log their hours of sleep. A streak and weekly milestone keep the habit going. Each metric has an info icon that explains why it is tracked, what each rating means, and what to do about it.

Smart interpretation of your data

AI turns lab results into something a person can read. Women upload a PDF or photo of a lab report, and Oviact lists each hormone with its trend. Tapping one shows the value beside the normal range, a six-month trend, why it matters, related symptoms and what she can do. It is AI for healthcare in a practical sense: the app interprets the data for her, so she goes into a consult informed instead of confused.

Exportable reports for clinical visits

One summary replaces the notes she wrote at 2 AM. A consult summary lets her choose what to share: symptom trends, latest lab results, plan adherence and questions for her doctor. A preview shows the overall status and key highlights before she exports it.

Partners are no longer bystanders

A shared roadmap brings partners into the plan. Both people see the fertility timeline, from the current test result to the next test and the egg-freezing window. A financial plan tracks combined savings, and a conversation starter helps them raise the next step together.

04ImpactWhy it matters, and where it fits

Impact

Oviact works in three layers, and each one has its own impact. Together they turn scattered symptom data into earlier answers and better care.

Personalised insightsContext-dependent insights help with early detection of diabetes, infertility and other risks.
Clinician-ready summariesOrganised summaries reduce delays in diagnosis and treatment.
Education and communityEvidence-based education and community support timely action and lifestyle changes.

Market

The global digital PCOS market is about $2.4B, and the US part is about $252M. Our first target is a $10M beachhead over 3 to 5 years: US women aged 20 to 35 who are digitally active and live in urban or suburban areas, reachable through PCOS communities, campuses and early employers.

Competitors

Most apps treat PCOS as a fertility problem. Oviact treats it as a lifelong health condition. Kindbody, Mira and Ovia Health offer treatment support, but around a fertility goal. Flo and Flo Living are basic logging tools for fertility. Allara and Perla address lifelong PCOS health but sit closer to basic logging. Oviact is the one combining treatment support with lifelong PCOS health.

Expert view

05ReflectionThe win, and what I took from it

The win

Oviact won second place at Hack-a-Startup, CMU’s startup competition run by the Swartz Center for Entrepreneurship. We had two weeks, a team of four, and a problem we had only just started to understand.

The result came from the work behind the product: a symptom board we ran with potential users at the CMU University Center, more than 21 interviews, over 100 Reddit comments, and a clinician’s view that the idea fills a blind spot in PCOS care. The app was built on top of that, not the other way round.

What I took from it

Frame the right problem before solving it. Our process wasn’t polished. We changed direction more than once and tested our thinking as much as the product, and that is what made the final idea clear.

I also learned how far talking to people early can take a team. Standing next to a board and listening to potential users gave us stories and patterns that no desk research could, and it shaped every screen I designed afterwards. Using vibe coding for the UI let me get those ideas in front of people fast, so the time went into research and decisions instead of production.

Privy

As a research assistant in CMU’s SPUD Lab, I’m designing Privy, an AI tool that walks product teams through finding and mitigating privacy risks before they ship.

LabSPUD Lab, CMU HCII
TeamHao-Ping (Hank) Lee, Sauvik Das
TimelineMay 2026 – present
ToolsFigmaProject site ↗
Problem
Product teams without privacy expertise struggle to find and reduce privacy risks before they ship.
What I did
Redesigning the full product with Hank Lee: the information architecture, the five-step interface and responses grounded in compliance documents.
Result

01ContextWhat Privy is and where I worked

Overview

Privy is an AI-powered privacy risk envisioning tool for AI product teams. It guides practitioners without privacy expertise through a structured privacy impact assessment: describe the product, find the privacy risks that matter, and plan how to reduce them, before anything is built.

Privy began as research in the SPUD Lab at Carnegie Mellon’s HCII, led by Hao-Ping (Hank) Lee with Sauvik Das, and was published at CHI ’26. In their study, privacy experts rated the assessments that non-expert practitioners produced with Privy as high quality. Project site ↗ · Paper ↗

I work with Hank on the product itself: the information architecture, the five-step interface, and, most importantly, grounding Privy’s responses in existing compliance documents, including work for an ed-tech client. I’m redesigning the whole product, starting with the Mitigate page.

Information architecture

Before redesigning screens, I mapped the whole product. A reviewer logs in, lands on a dashboard of PIA reports, and opens one in a workspace with four steps: Identify, Rank, Mitigate and Summarise. Export then leads to a Knowledge Hub, where engineering, product, design and legal teams each answer the questions that apply to them.

02GroundingMaking Privy answer like the documents do

Grounding Privy in compliance documents

Key contribution

I grounded Privy’s responses in existing compliance documents. For each Privy input, such as product name, purpose, data, and how a user might interact with the product, I set the original review-form question beside each product’s answer and wrote the response Privy should produce from it.

This is what lets Privy answer the way an organisation’s own documents do, instead of the way a general-purpose model would. It tailors the LLM’s outputs, and it gives engineers the groundwork to code and prompt Privy’s outputs against.
03The productFive steps, from first description to final report

The product, step by step

Each step answers one question: what could go wrong, which risks come first, what do we do about them, and what do we hand over.

Log in and dashboard

Reviewers sign in, or continue as a guest, and land on a dashboard of their reports. Each card shows its status and dates: Edit Report to keep working, View PIA Report once it’s complete.

Step 1: Identify

The assessment is a canvas that reads left to right. The team describes the product. Privy generates use cases, each tagged as intended or unintended, then the capabilities and requirements behind them, and finally the privacy risks they create. Every risk carries a type, a relevancy and severity rating, and a way to see who is impacted. Teams can also write their own risk and assess it in two steps.

Step 2: Rank

Reviewers drag risks into a priority order. Relevancy and severity stay visible on every card, so the ordering is easy to defend.

Step 3: Mitigate

Each risk gets its own brainstorm. A risk list sits on the left. In the centre, control principles such as notice, consent, purpose limitation and access control prompt a question, and Privy can generate a mitigation idea in response. Good ideas are added to the mitigation plan on the right, and reviewers rate their confidence in the plan, or ask Privy for feedback.

Work in progress. I’m redesigning the whole product, starting with this page, so its styling differs from the rest.

Step 4: Summarise

The report brings the assessment into one view. It opens with the highlighted risks, how relevancy and severity are distributed, and how confident the team is in each mitigation. Below that, every section is editable in place before Save & Export.

Knowledge Hub

After export, teams work through the compliance questions a corporate PIA needs. Compliance Gaps tracks how many items are addressed and filters them by unanswered, answered, AI-extrapolated and system default. Each question is assigned to a team, and Privy AI can pre-fill answers.

Work in progress. The Knowledge Hub is being redesigned too.

04StatusWhere this stands

Still in progress

I’ve been designing Privy since May and I’m still working on it. I’m redesigning the whole product, beginning with the Mitigate page and the Knowledge Hub. User tests are next, and I’ll add what I learn from them here.
